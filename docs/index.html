Corpus construction · UK television advertising · BFI National Archive

Cutting each advert out of the broadcast it aired in decides what the corpus contains

The BFI delivered continuous recordings of UK television with industry metadata, but nothing in a file says where an advert begins. This page reports how single adverts are cut out of those recordings, what the first full run produced, what is lost, and what is still open. It accompanies Chapter 4 of the thesis.

1 · The material

The recordings are continuous, and the metadata keeps a different clock

DeliveryThe first BFI delivery, chosen by a script so that every advert identifier appears at least once1
RecordingA continuous broadcast, tens of minutes to three hours, programmes and breaks in turn1,670
BreakListed in the metadata with clock times, never with a position in the file11,254
RecordOne row per advert broadcast: time, place in break, brand, identifier97,213
ClipOne advert cut out of the recording, with one row in the processing log. This is the study's work.25,208cut points, Sept run

The metadata gives clock times, such as 20:46:20, while a file counts from zero, and nothing records when a recording began. The time in a file's name is the start of one break, not the start of the recording. The two clocks have to be matched before the metadata can be used at all, and even then its times are given to the second, while a cut point has to be placed to the frame (25 a second).

The identifier states a length. Its last three digits are the advert's length in seconds: where an advert is followed by another in its break, the next one starts exactly that many seconds later in 99.6% of 62,001 cases. This is read from the data, not from documentation, and it is what lets every clip be checked against a length known in advance.

Selection matters. The recordings were chosen to cover every identifier, not at random, so the broadcast an advert is cut from is not a typical one. Channel and time of day are therefore always taken from the full broadcast history in the metadata, never from the clip.

Opening of one break (Channel 4, 29 April 2024), identifiers shortened. Struck-through rows are placeholders; one "place" is a product category. The gap equals the last three digits of the identifier.

Each identifier is one advert

Ten- and thirty-second versions under the same brand carry different identifiers, and are counted as different adverts.

Placeholder rows are removed, not cut

Addressable advertising is sold per household. Three placeholder identifiers mark these slots in 25,975 of the 97,213 records (about 27%). All but three share a start time with a real advert, which keeps the boundary.

What the metadata omits is not in the corpus

A sponsor's advert shown just before a break has no record, so it is not cut.

3 · The procedure

Sound finds the join, the schedule decides whether to believe it, the picture sets the frame

audio

List the silences

Adverts are delivered with six frames of silence at each end, so two in a row are separated by about half a second of silence (median 0.645 s between adverts).

schedule

Match the fingerprint

The gaps between a break's adverts, say 20, 30, 10 and 20 s, are slid along the recording until every boundary falls on a silence.

schedule

Keep or drop the break

A break is kept only if every boundary matches and its offset agrees with the break kept before it. One miss drops the whole break.

picture

Place the cut

From the silence midpoint, the cut moves to the first abrupt picture change. A guardrail reverts it if it lands more than two frames after sound returns.

No model touches the boundaries. Segmentation is deterministic signal processing; a vision-language model enters only afterwards, to describe finished clips. The full walkthrough, with one break worked through under three rule sets, is in the pipeline explainer. Settings for each run are in Method and settings.

4 · Validation

Three kinds of evidence, each blind where another can see

every cut · automatic

Stated length

Clip length against the length in its own identifier. Tests the distance between neighbouring cuts.

Blind to a shift shared by every cut.

every cut · automatic

Return of sound

Where the cut sits against the end of the silence. Across the full run the median cut falls 277 ms before sound returns.

Cannot tell a late cut from sound that starts late.

sample · by hand

Frame by frame

The only check that fixes the position of a cut, and the brand label. 23 cut points so far.

Too few to report an accuracy figure.

One case needed all three. Two clips came out at 19.3 s and 20.7 s where both should have been 20 s; the audio showed one cut 371 ms after sound had returned; stepping through the frames showed the refinement had pushed the cut 17 frames into the next advert. The guardrail was written in response.

6 · Coverage

About two thirds of the unique adverts were obtained, and the losses may not fall evenly

19,503 unique adverts in the first delivery · audit of 10 October 2026
12,409 obtained
3,994 missing, aired once
3,100 other missing

For the 3,994 adverts that appear only once in the delivery, a lost slot is a lost advert. The counts are a baseline: both were made when only one of the three placeholder types was recognised, and will be made again.

The slot was never in the file

The metadata lists breaks that are not in the recording, and for 327 files (19.6%) it mixes in the breaks of a second channel.

The metadata groups its records wrongly

Breaks split in two at the same second (1,758 and 958 breaks), and two breaks written as one with a gap of minutes inside (200). These are now repaired before alignment.

The strict rule drops whole breaks

One missed boundary removes a break of eight or nine adverts, and the offset check removes most breaks placed in the wrong position.

Why it matters for the analysis. A loss that fell evenly would only make the corpus smaller; a loss that falls unevenly changes its shape. The next full run will report retention by year, advert length and channel before any distribution is compared.
